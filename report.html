How a catalog learns to file itself

Four years of product data work, and the arithmetic that makes small improvements matter.

Van Sirisouk New York October 2026 3 minute read

Abstract

Between 2023 and 2025 I worked on the product catalog behind Walmart's e-commerce site: reconciling supplier data, removing duplicate records, and automating a submission process that people had been doing by hand.

The results read like small numbers. Queries ran 30% faster. Sessions got 22% shorter. A pipeline processed 450,000 records a second. The figures below show why they matter at catalog scale, and each one states the assumption it rests on.

1The same shirt, six ways

A catalog is not a list. It is an agreement about what each thing is. Clothing breaks that agreement first: one style becomes two products by color and six SKUs by size, and every supplier names them differently.

2Three systems

2.1Cleaning

Years of failed submissions leave duplicates. The service merges them in place, per tenant, and writes an audit trail, so queries stop scanning rows that say the same thing twice.

2.2Intake

Layouts arrived as files and were filed by hand. The pipeline validates and publishes one on arrival, at 450,000 records a second.

2.3Submissions

External sellers had no clean way in, so edge cases became tickets. The app validates and previews before it writes, and the average session got 22% shorter. On a form, shorter means fewer dead ends.

3What it adds up to

At 390,000 submissions a day, the app handles about 142 million a year. That is the multiplier. A quarter of that traffic, six minutes a session, and the 22% is worth roughly 780,000 hours.

The model below makes the last two numbers yours to change.

4Method

Measurement and projection are different things. So are estimates.

Measured
30% faster queries, $1,200 a year in storage, 450,000 records a second, 390,000 submissions a day, and 22% shorter sessions. Source: internal dashboards at the time, and my notes.
Derived
Annual submissions (daily × 365). Filing time (records × 20 seconds). Hours returned (volume × human share × session × 22%). Arithmetic on measured inputs, not new measurements.
Assumed
20 seconds per record. 25% human traffic. Six minutes per session. Figure 5 exposes the last two as controls.
Not measured
Revenue. Attribution was not mine to claim, and a dollar figure would have been invented.
